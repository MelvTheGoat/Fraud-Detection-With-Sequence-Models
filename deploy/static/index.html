Why some values say “new”

The model learned from a simulated card portfolio covering eight countries: the UK, Ireland, France, Germany, Spain, the Netherlands, Poland and the US. Anything outside what it saw — Nigeria, Kenya, a merchant code it has never met, a typo — lands in a single “never seen this before” slot.

So it does not know Nigeria specifically. NG, KE, ZA and a nonsense code like XX all produce exactly the same score, because to the model they are the same thing: unfamiliar. That is still useful — unfamiliarity is a genuine fraud signal, and the model uses it — but it is not country knowledge, and treating it as such would be wrong.

Making it actually understand Nigerian spending means retraining on Nigerian data. That is a real piece of work, not a setting.

How it decides

It reads the last 32 payments on the card, in order, and asks what this new one looks like given that sequence. Not rules like “over £500 is risky” — it compares against how this particular person normally spends: their usual amounts, their merchants, their devices, how fast they usually pay.

That is why changing one payment often barely moves the score, while the two burst buttons move it a long way. Real card fraud comes in runs, and runs are what the model is built to catch.

The two cut-offs were not picked for neatness. They come from costing mistakes: roughly £8 of damage for wrongly blocking a good customer, and £15 plus the money itself for letting fraud through. The thresholds are whatever minimised that total.

How good is it, and on what data

The data is synthetic. Every payment here comes from a simulator, not a real card portfolio. The figures describe that simulator and say nothing about real-world fraud.
